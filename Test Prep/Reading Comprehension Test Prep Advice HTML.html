Reading Comprehension: What the Prep Books Actually Advise

Kaplan, Princeton Review, Piqosity, PrepMaven and others agree on a core routine — predict in your own words, prove every answer from the text, eliminate traps by name, and never let one question eat the section. They disagree on one thing: whether to read the passage or the questions first. That disagreement resolves by test format, not philosophy — and the guessing rule is different on the SSAT than everywhere else.

01

The one real disagreement: questions first or passage first?

No source claims one order wins for every test. The deciding factor is how long the passage is and how many questions hang off it — short passage, few questions: you can hold the task in mind. Long passage, ten questions: you can't.

Questions first

Short passage · 1–6 questions

  • Digital SAT (1 short passage, 1 question): Kaplan — “Because each passage only has one question, reading the question before you jump into the passage will allow you to focus in on exactly what you need.” Princeton Review agrees: read the stem to label the task, then read only what that question needs.
  • ISEE (≈400 words, 6 questions): Piqosity — read the questions first, but not the answer choices; jot notes about what to look for, then read actively.
  • SSAT (≈250–350 words, 5 questions): PrepMaven — preview the questions before actively reading so you know what to look for.

Passage first

Long passage · 10 questions

  • ACT (~750 words, 10 questions): Kaplan calls questions-first “a common myth” — “It is almost impossible to remember to look for the answer to 10 different questions while reading an unfamiliar passage containing 750 words and trying to synthesize the big ideas.” Read for main idea and purpose first, take paragraph notes, then use line references and keywords to locate details.
  • CollegeMatchPoint/ATX makes the same practical case for the ACT: questions-first was a 1980s Princeton Review innovation weakened by later format changes.
Tutor move: don’t settle this by argument — test it. Run the same student through two timed passage sets of equal difficulty, once questions-first and once passage-first, and compare accuracy and time left. Keep the winner for that student. Defaults if you skip the experiment: digital SAT → questions-first; ACT → passage-first; ISEE/SSAT → questions skimmed, stems only, no choices.

Outdated SAT advice is still circulating — don’t teach it

Anything quoting a 65-minute section, 5 long passages, 52 questions, “13 minutes per passage,” or “detail questions follow passage order” describes the pre-2024 SAT. The current digital SAT is 54 questions in two 32-minute modules with short passages and one question each. Several otherwise useful pages (Princeton Review’s older tips page, CollegeMatchPoint, Kaplan’s older study guide) mix the two formats — check the format before quoting timing from them.

The numbers: pacing budgets and guessing rules

Teach these figures, not old-format ones. The guessing rule is the single most test-specific fact on this page — it reverses on the SSAT.

Guessing rules — they differ by test, teach them exactly

TestRule
SAT · ACT · ISEENo wrong-answer penalty — answer every question. If you skip one, enter a guess immediately rather than leaving it for later. Kaplan ACT’s version: pre-pick a “Letter of the Day” (e.g. A/F or B/G) for blind guesses. Piqosity on the ISEE: “DO NOT leave it blank.”
SSAT Middle / Upper−¼ point per wrong answer; blank = 0. Guess only if you can eliminate at least one choice (Kaplan SSAT; Prepped & Polished says guess if you can eliminate 1–2). Elementary SSAT has no penalty — answer everything there.

A pacing line to avoid repeating

Prepped & Polished’s “about a minute per passage and a minute to a minute and a half per question” doesn’t survive arithmetic: applied to the ISEE (6 passages × 6 questions) it exceeds the 35-minute section. It’s flagged in the research so tutors don’t pass it on.

Before reading the passage

Digital SAT

Read the blurb first

On SAT passages — especially literature — read the italic intro first: author, year, source, context. It frames everything that follows and costs seconds.

ISEE

Bank the no-reading questions first

When you skim the questions, answer any vocabulary or figurative-language questions that need no passage reading before you read. It banks points and reduces the reading load.

Work in your strongest order, not printed order

Start with your strongest passage type (Kaplan ACT). On the SSAT, tackle what’s easiest first and take passages out of order (PrepMaven). On the ISEE, judge “will this passage take me a long time?” and save longer, harder ones for last (Piqosity).

04

While reading: annotate for retrieval, not decoration

Every mark a student makes must help them find an answer later. That is the shared principle behind every publisher’s annotation advice.

ISEESSATACT

A few words per paragraph, in your own words

Paraphrase each paragraph’s main idea in the margin — “more than just underlining” (PrepMaven). Kaplan ACT: short notes on topic and purpose, and keep track of different people and opinions. On the ISEE, you’re allowed to write all over the test booklet — take advantage of it (Piqosity).

PrepMaven · Kaplan ACT · Piqosity · Boston Tutoring Services
SSATACT

Map the passage

Visualize the passage as a map: Where is the thesis sentence? Underline it. What is the structure as a whole? Read for main ideas, statements of opinion or argument, transition words, keywords from the questions, and concluding statements.

PrepMaven, 6 SSAT Reading Strategies
All tests

“What it says” / “What it does”

After each paragraph, say in one sentence what it says, and what it does — “provides evidence for the author’s first main reason,” “introduces an opposing view.” This general active-reading move (Princeton’s McGraw Center; SkillsYouNeed) is the mechanism underneath all the publisher annotation advice, and it transfers to every test.

Princeton McGraw Center · SkillsYouNeed
All tests

What not to do

  • Don’t read every word slowly to “learn” the passage — focus on answering questions, not studying the text, and don’t keep rereading parts you don’t understand (Prepped & Polished; Princeton Review).
  • Don’t highlight everything. “Put down your highlighter. Make marginal notes instead” (Princeton McGraw).
  • Don’t rely on memory instead of going back: “Memories can be faulty… line references are provided for a reason” (Kaplan ACT).

Paired passages

Two passages? One at a time

Do Passage 1’s questions first, then Passage 2’s, and the both-passage questions last — it saves time and keeps you from confusing the two texts (Princeton Review). On the digital SAT each question has its own short passage or short pair, so tailor your reading to that one question and use only that excerpt (Kaplan). Before answering any comparison question, be able to state each author’s claim in 5–7 words and whether they agree, disagree, or extend — that formulation comes from secondary SAT guides and matches Princeton Review’s one-at-a-time rule, but isn’t publisher-verified.

Princeton Review · Kaplan · secondary guides (comparison rule)
05

The hard-passage routine: hunt the main idea, slog without stalling

For the passage that looks like a wall — dense, unfamiliar, easy to zone out on. This routine is assembled from the moves in §04 and §06, sequenced the way a tutor would drill them; each step is sourced, the sequence is the drill.

Part A — Hunt the main idea before you read deeply

  1. Read the frame first: the blurb (author, year, source) and the first and last paragraphs. Conclusions and topic sentences are where main ideas live (Kaplan SAT; PrepMaven).
  2. Find and underline the thesis sentence. Then ask PrepMaven’s map questions: What is the structure as a whole? Where do the ideas turn? The signal words in §04 mark the turns — a however or therefore usually carries more weight than a full paragraph of examples.
  3. Say the main idea in one sentence, in your own words, before answering anything. “If you could summarize a passage in one sentence, that sentence would be the main idea” (Union Test Prep). CollegeMatchPoint’s version: the summary you make to yourself after a first reading is your main-idea answer.

Part B — Work through the dense parts in chunks

  1. One paragraph at a time, pen moving. After each paragraph, write the margin note: what it says in a few words, and what it does (evidence, example, opposing view, conclusion). The note is the checkpoint — if you can’t write it, you drifted, and you re-read that one paragraph now, while it’s cheap (Princeton McGraw; SkillsYouNeed).
  2. Track people and opinions as separate entries. Dense passages usually tangle who thinks what, not vocabulary. Kaplan ACT: keep track of different people and opinions in your notes as you go.
  3. When a sentence won’t yield, mark it and keep moving. Put a symbol in the margin and read on — later sentences often define or simplify the hard one, and the questions may never touch it. Prepped & Polished is blunt: “Do not keep rereading portions that you don’t understand.” Rereading a stuck sentence three times is how a passage eats its whole time budget (§02).
  4. Answer from the map, hunt details only when asked. Detail questions send you back with keywords as a scavenger hunt (§06); don’t re-read the whole passage hunting for one fact — go to the paragraph your map says holds it, read that sentence plus its context, and answer (PrepMaven; Kaplan ACT).

Part C — Staying engaged instead of zoning out

  • Give the pen a job on every paragraph. The annotation routine above doubles as the attention routine: zoning out is what happens between checkpoints, and a required margin note every paragraph makes drift visible immediately instead of a page later.
  • Predict as you read. Before each new paragraph, guess where the author is going — agreement, objection, example? Piqosity and PrepMaven both prescribe prediction during pleasure reading as the way the skill is built; on the test, a wrong prediction still keeps you reading against the text instead of sliding over it.
  • Use the questions as anchors where the format allows. On questions-first tests (digital SAT, ISEE, SSAT — §01), you already know what you’re hunting; on the ACT, your paragraph map serves the same anchoring job when you reach the questions.
  • Treat fading stamina as a training problem, not a test-day problem. Princeton Review: build it in practice — module-length timed bursts on weeknights, one full section on the weekend, brief reset between modules — so a hard passage late in the section meets a reader who has rehearsed exactly that fatigue.
06

By question type: the concrete move for each

The universal first move — predict, then prove

Every publisher converges here. Kaplan SAT calls it Predict-and-Match; Kaplan ACT: “Predict an answer BEFORE you look at the answer choices.” Princeton Review: prediction in plain language plus proof — “If you can’t point to that support, you’re not done.” PrepMaven SSAT: cover the choices and predict. Tutor drill: cover the choices with a hand or card until the student says a prediction aloud. If they can’t predict, they haven’t located the evidence yet — send them back to the passage; don’t let them shop the choices.

All tests

Main idea / primary purpose / best title

  • Summarize the whole passage in one sentence — “If you could summarize a passage in one sentence, that sentence would be the main idea” (Union Test Prep, ISEE).
  • Reject choices that cover only one paragraph or example (too narrow) or import ideas the passage never raises (too broad). Piqosity’s worked example: a title fitting only the first part or the last sentences is wrong; the right one captures overall scope.
  • Source the main idea from thesis and topic sentences and concluding statements (PrepMaven).
  • Within a passage, save main-idea questions for last — after the detail questions, you know the themes (Princeton Review). A within-passage ordering rule, not a section rule.
Union Test Prep · Princeton Review · PrepMaven · Piqosity
All tests

Detail / locate information

  • Turn the question’s keywords into a scavenger hunt: find the keyword in the passage, read that sentence plus its context, then answer (PrepMaven).
  • For a line reference, read around it — lines 10–14 for a line-12 question (Kaplan ACT).
  • Digital SAT evidence questions: check the sentence plus one line before and after. The right evidence must directly prove the claim, not merely mention the topic — “If I quoted this sentence, would it make the claim obviously true?” (Princeton Review).
  • A detail answer is directly stated; no inference is needed. If you’re inferring, you’re probably answering the wrong question type.
PrepMaven · Kaplan ACT · Princeton Review · Piqosity
All tests

Inference / implication

  • Stated facts plus one logical step — never outside knowledge, never a second leap. PrepMaven: “SSAT inferences rarely take too many steps away from the passage.” Its example: “Bailey enjoys traveling” supports “finds pleasure in it,” not “enjoys traveling in Europe.”
  • If it’s directly stated, it is not an inference — teach students to separate stated from implied explicitly (Union Test Prep; Piqosity).
  • Prefer the weaker, hedged inference that matches the passage’s strength. Watch the cause-vs-correlation trap: without because or therefore, prefer “association” over “causes” (Princeton Review).
Princeton Review · PrepMaven · Union Test Prep · Piqosity
All tests

Vocabulary in context — the Blank-It method

  • Cover the target word. Read the sentence (and a line around it) without it. Predict your own simple word that fits the meaning and the tone. Only then look at the choices.
  • Don’t plug choices in first — Princeton Review warns that doing so makes distractors feel “right.”
  • Use context clues, not the most familiar dictionary meaning: synonyms/antonyms, examples or definitions in the sentence, roots and affixes. More than one choice may technically work — pick the best fit and eliminate by connotation (Union Test Prep’s example: odor/fumes are negative; fresh pies get aroma).
  • Even if you’re 100% sure you know the word, check the passage — it may be used non-traditionally (PrepMaven). If two SAT choices are close, pick the one matching the passage’s tone and strength (Princeton Review).
Princeton Review + Kaplan (convergent) · Union Test Prep · PrepMaven · Piqosity
All tests

Tone / purpose / function / structure

  • Function questions: label what the sentence or paragraph does — define, give an example, contrast, qualify, concede, conclude — then pick the choice naming that job (Princeton Review).
  • Tone comes from the author’s specific adjectives, verbs, and connotations, not from the topic (PrepMaven: “thrill / unexpected” → suspense and urgency; Piqosity: negative-connotation words signal dislike). Avoid extreme tone words.
  • Link purpose and tone: if the purpose is “to explain,” the tone is likely informative or interested, not angry or mocking. Genre is a useful prior — scientific study → “to explain,” editorial → “to persuade” — but verify against the actual passage (PrepMaven).
  • Structure questions (ISEE): name the pattern — sequence, comparison, cause/effect — and ask why the author presented the ideas in this order (Piqosity). A Venn diagram is a good practice drill for compare/contrast.
  • Figurative language (ISEE/SSAT): learn the devices before test day — simile (like/as), metaphor, personification, hyperbole, irony, imagery — and ask what mood or theme the device creates, not what it literally says (Piqosity).
Princeton Review · PrepMaven · Piqosity · Union Test Prep
All tests

EXCEPT / NOT / “all of the following”

  • Invert the task: strike off every choice you do find in the passage. The one remaining is the answer (PrepMaven, for EXCEPT questions).
  • Re-read the stem for NOT/EXCEPT before answering — this is one of the most common careless-error catches in review.
  • On the ACT, older guidance says to skip EXCEPT and Roman-numeral questions and do them last — teach that as a pacing choice, not a content rule.
PrepMaven · secondary ACT guidance
07

Trap answers: teach students to name the trap

The same traps appear in Kaplan’s ACT advice, Princeton Review’s digital SAT advice, and PrepMaven’s SSAT advice — that convergence across publishers is strong evidence they’re test-wide, not one book’s folklore. A choice the student can name is a choice they can discard.

TrapHow to spot itThe counter-move
Extreme / absolute languagealways, never, all, none, best, worst, completely, must, impossibleEliminate unless the passage is equally absolute. Mirror the passage’s hedging: if the text says may / can / often, the answer must too.
Half-right, half-wrongStarts accurately, then tacks on an unsupported phraseRead to the end of the choice. If any part overreaches, the whole choice is wrong — one word can sink it.
Out-of-scope / unsupportedA concept, fact, or motive that never appears in the passageAsk: “Can I point to a line that backs this?” If not, eliminate it.
Opposite / reverse logicSame keywords, flipped meaning; cause and effect swappedRestate the author’s point in your own words first, then compare meaning, not wording.
Misused / distorted detailA true statement from the passage that answers a different question, or keywords reused misleadinglyAsk: “Is this true and does it answer this question?” Both must hold.
Too broad / too narrowScope drift — a main-idea choice covering one example, or a whole category the passage never discussesMatch exact scope: time, place, group, degree, condition.
Echo wordingBorrows the passage’s vocabulary but subtly changes the claim or scopeMatch ideas, not words. Familiar phrasing is a warning sign, not a recommendation.
Cause vs. correlationThe passage shows two things occurring together; the choice claims one causes the otherRequire a causal signal (because, therefore); otherwise prefer the “association” wording.
Tone / purpose mismatchA neutral or cautiously positive passage paired with a negative or emphatic answer (or the reverse)Check tone and purpose alignment before locking anything in.

The elimination routine — teach it verbatim

  1. Predict first.
  2. Cross out every choice with a demonstrable flaw — physically, in the booklet or with the digital strikethrough tool.
  3. Compare survivors on the small scope words — only, primarily, may, often, because (Princeton Review).
  4. Choose the best answer, not the most intelligent-sounding one. The correct answer “will be flawless” and fully supported (Kaplan ACT).
  5. If two remain, pick the one with the shortest, clearest, closest textual support — the fewest assumptions.
08

Pacing, minute by minute

Digital SAT

Two passes per module

Pass 1 (≈18–20 min): quick wins only — clear grammar, simple vocab, obvious main idea or function. Flag anything slow. Pass 2: work the flagged questions methodically. Final 60–90 seconds: confirm every question is filled. Hard cap: if a question won’t budge in 40–50 seconds, flag it and move on. In practice, use a 32-minute timer and aim to be 2–3 questions ahead at the midpoint.

Princeton Review, SAT Reading and Writing Tips
ACT

Four 8½-minute units

Treat the section as four units of ~8–9 minutes, one per passage, and finish each passage’s questions inside its unit — returning later forces expensive re-reading. Never spend the whole section on one passage; move on at the budget. Take the passages in any order, strongest first.

Kaplan ACT · ACT pacing guidance
ISEE

Order by reading cost

35 minutes, 6 passages. Before starting each passage, judge “Will this passage take me a long time to read?” Save the longer and harder ones for last. And never leave a blank — there is no penalty.

Piqosity
SSAT

Accuracy over completion

40 minutes, 40 questions. Easiest passages and questions first. If a question stalls around 45–60 seconds, mark it, guess only if you’ve eliminated at least one choice, and move on. Second pass: marked questions first. Protect the final 60–90 seconds to bubble anything with at least one elimination — and leave only untouched questions blank.

PrepMaven · SSAT penalty-strategy guides
All tests

Train the clock, don’t just meet it on test day

Practice under timed conditions periodically with full sections, not only untimed drills. Princeton Review’s shape: module-length bursts on weeknights plus one full section on the weekend, in a quiet room, with an on-screen timer and a brief reset between modules. Stamina problems are practice problems — they’re fixed in training, not on test day.

Princeton Review · Kaplan
09

When stuck: a four-step script to run without thinking

Synthesized from Princeton Review’s “If You Freeze” guidance, Kaplan’s triage system, and Piqosity’s skip-and-return rule — each step is sourced; the combination is the script to drill until it’s automatic.

If you’re behind on time

Cap the next two tricky questions at 60 seconds each: eliminate, choose the most supportable answer, and finish the set. Then use the final two minutes to fill blanks and revisit only quickly-solvable flagged items. Princeton Review

If two choices feel identical

Translate the question into your own words, re-read only the tied lines, eliminate anything extreme or off-scope, predict, mark, and move. Princeton Review

If English isn’t the student’s first language

Read two lines around the target word, blank it out, predict a simple word, eliminate non-matches — and guess and move if you can’t narrow it to one. Between sessions, build a small deck of academic and tone words, each with the student’s own example sentence. Princeton Review

Reviewing mistakes: the highest-leverage tutoring work

Princeton Review’s error-log routine is the most detailed in the sources. It’s written for the SAT but the routine is test-agnostic — apply it to ISEE, SSAT, and ACT prep unchanged.

The error log — one row per miss or guess

DateSectionQuestion type / skillSourceYour answer + whyCorrect answerError type / causeKey takeawayReviewed?
——e.g. InferencePractice test / setChoice + the reasoning usedCorrect choiceContent · Process · Pacing · Careless · Misunderstood logic · GuessedThe rule or reading move to use next time☐

Copies as tab-separated text that pastes straight into a spreadsheet. Template columns: Princeton Review.

After every set — the four-step review

  1. Fill the log for every miss and every guess.
  2. Re-solve missed items untimed until the reasoning is airtight.
  3. Create two same-type practice items for tomorrow.
  4. Two days later, retest that skill timed.

Weekly shape: Day 1, a baseline full section and categorize the misses; reading-focus days on Function/Evidence, then Words-in-Context/Tone; a mixed timed set; Day 7, a full-section retest — compare accuracy by skill and plan next week around the top two weaknesses. Princeton Review

All tests

Classify every mistake by cause, then target the cause

Vocabulary gap, comprehension error, or careless mistake? Each has a different fix, so name it in the log and target that specific weakness in the next session. Track weak question types — inference? main idea? vocabulary? — and drill the weakest. And review explanations for correct answers too, not just wrong ones: a guessed-correct answer is still a gap.

TestPrep-Online (ISEE) · consistent with Princeton Review’s log
All tests

Benchmark, build stamina, don’t just take more tests

Take at least one benchmark test before test day, and build up section-by-section before full-length tests so the full exam “feels manageable, not overwhelming” (TestPrep-Online). Piqosity’s claim that multiple practice exams improve performance is Piqosity’s — treat it as their assertion, not independent research. A secondary SAT guide puts the balance bluntly: spend 2–3 days reviewing each full test before taking another; mastery happens when you correct old mistakes, not when you add new tests.

Piqosity · TestPrep-Online · secondary SAT guide
All tests

Read for fun — with intention

Read widely and eclectically at or slightly above the student’s level: articles, editorials, journal pieces, older texts and primary documents, poetry, creative nonfiction, science and history writing. While reading for fun, run the test skills without questions guiding you: define new words, summarize each chapter before moving on, predict what happens next, annotate, invent your own questions, infer from characterization and tone. Kaplan ACT: “try to explain the authors’ findings to a friend.” Kaplan’s enrichment version: pick a news article, write SAT-style questions for it, and swap with a study partner — it teaches how test-makers build wrong answers.

Piqosity · PrepMaven · Kaplan ACT · Kaplan study guide

The three disagreements, resolved

  • Questions-first vs passage-first: resolved by format, not philosophy (§01). Short passage + few questions → questions-first. Long passage + 10 questions → passage-first. Test both with the individual student.
  • Read every word vs read what you need: every current publisher source favors targeted reading. The partial dissent — read a paragraph fully before highlighting or summarizing it — is about study reading, not timed test reading. Don’t conflate them.
  • Finish everything vs prioritize accuracy: on no-penalty tests (SAT/ACT/ISEE), finish everything and guess freely. On the SSAT, accuracy wins and unfinished is fine. Never teach one guessing rule for both.

Open questions — what this research could not verify

Named plainly so nothing below gets quoted as settled advice.

  • Test Prep Works (ISEE/SSAT books): its books exist and are noted as not produced or endorsed by ERB, but no specific Test Prep Works reading strategy was found in an accessible primary source. Nothing on this page is attributed to them.
  • Barron’s (SAT/ACT): searches surfaced only aggregator and pirated PDFs, which were not used. No Barron’s-specific reading advice is reported.
  • Ivy Global (SSAT/ISEE): only test-format details were verified (e.g. that you may write in the ISEE test booklet). No Ivy Global strategy chapter was accessible.
  • ISEE Lower Level passage count — a live discrepancy: Piqosity says Lower Level is 5 passages × 5 questions; an Ivy Global Lower Level sample shows 6 passages. Confirm current level specs against official ERB/ISEE materials before teaching passage counts.
  • College Board: no College Board–authored strategy page was read in full in this research; it appears only via publisher and secondary references to official practice tests and the Khan Academy collaboration. Don’t quote College Board as the direct source for a specific tactic based on this page.
  • Kaplan’s SSAT guessing rule (guess if you can eliminate ≥1 choice) rests on a search-result snippet rather than a full page read; it is corroborated by Prepped & Polished and SSAT penalty guides.
